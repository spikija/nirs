
The authors investigate whether NIRS can identify cerebral ischaemia during mechanical thrombectomy and detect reperfusion-related changes. This is an interesting and clinically relevant question. However, I have a major general concern regarding the level of analysis and interpretation.
As a clinical anaesthesiologist, I need monitoring to provide useful information for the individual patient, not merely an average association in a study population. This is also what the stated aims imply: whether NIRS can “reliably identify the affected hemisphere” and capture recanalization-related changes. These are questions of individual-patient performance, rather than population-level associations.
However, all current analyses are conducted and reported at the group level. They show average differences between hemispheres or time periods, but do not show whether the lower rSO₂ value identifies the stroke side in the patient in front of us, or whether a meaningful change reliably occurs when reperfusion is achieved. The current analyses therefore cannot support the proposed clinical monitoring application. Please provide patient-level analyses or moderate the aims and conclusions accordingly.
Specifically:
1•       Evaluate and display individual affected-hemisphere identification. 
Report the number and percentage of patients in whom rSO₂ was lower, equal or higher 
on the affected side. 
Show the raw affected–unaffected rSO₂ difference for every patient in a dot plot, 
with zero indicating no asymmetry. 
In addition, report how often the hemisphere with the lower rSO₂ value correctly 
corresponded to the angiographically affected hemisphere, including correct, incorrect and 
indeterminate classifications with 95% CI. 
Otherwise, remove “reliably identifies” from the aims and conclusions.

2•       Show individual changes around reperfusion. 
Provide paired dot-and-line plots for every patient, showing rSO₂ before and after 
angiographic reperfusion/final MT attempt, separately for affected and unaffected hemispheres. 
This will show whether the average increase reflects a consistent individual response.

3•       Compare temporal changes directly between hemispheres. 
Calculate patient-level post–pre ΔrSO₂ for both hemispheres and test whether the 
change differs between sides. Show these paired changes graphically and 
report the median paired change, IQR and 95% CI. 
A significant change on one side and a non-significant change on the other does not 
demonstrate a between-side difference.

4•       Test reperfusion specificity directly. 
Compare patient-level NIRS changes in patients with successful versus unsuccessful reperfusion. 
If available, align the analysis to angiographic confirmation of reperfusion rather than 
merely the final MT attempt.

A: i do not think we have enough patients to make meaningful comparison (only 19 patients with 0-2a)

5•       Test subgroup differences directly. 
For each patient, calculate the relevant NIRS effect 
(e.g. affected–unaffected difference or post–pre change) 
and compare this effect between good versus poor collaterals and 
successful versus unsuccessful reperfusion. 
Separate subgroup p-values do not demonstrate effect modification.

- again there is no value in comparing successful vs. unsuccessful reperfusion

6•       Address multiplicity. 
Define a primary analysis and label the remaining temporal and subgroup analyses as 
exploratory. Avoid basing conclusions on isolated p-values.

•       Make NIRS preprocessing reproducible. Specify objective artefact criteria, 
the minimum valid data required per window, handling of missing data and sensor displacement, 
whether reviewers were blinded, and the number of excluded observations.

7•       Clarify possible treatment influence. State whether clinicians could see the NIRS display and whether it could have influenced blood-pressure, ventilation or anaesthetic management.
A: the NIRS data were visible, treatment decisions could have been influenced based on NIRS data.

The authors could, of course, address all these issues by explicitly limiting all aims and conclusions to population-level associations. However, this would substantially reduce the clinical usefulness and novelty of the study. Demonstrating a small average difference between groups is not the same as demonstrating that NIRS can function as a clinically useful monitoring tool during MT. If the intended contribution is individualised intraprocedural monitoring, the requested patient-level analyses are needed.
